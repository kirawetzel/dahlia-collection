Growing in Zone 9B

San Jose's Zone 9B is one of the best climates on earth for dahlias and year round gardening with long, warm summers, mild winters, and very little frost risk mean tubers can stay in the ground year-round, although I typically dig at the end of the season. I usually try to plant my dahlias in February and March to see the first blooms in April or May, with peak production running August through October, and the plants keep going well into November. In 2026 I had a late start

The tradeoff is heat management in July and August, when temperatures can stress plants during bud formation. I mulch heavily using rice straw to maintain soil health and regulate temperatures during heat waves.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

I use only OMRI listed (Organic Materials Review Institute) products. Rather than reaching for pesticides, both gardens run on a biological IPM program. The philosophy is to build a system that manages itself, beneficial predators doing the work so I don't have to. Additionally, because I mix food and flowers in both gardens, it's important to make sure I'm not using anything that could be really harmful to people.

Beneficial insect releases (current program):
  • Lacewings, generalist predators; effective against thrips, aphids, whitefly, and small caterpillars at the larval stage
  • Minute pirate bugs, highly aggressive thrips predators; faster-acting than lacewings and effective across all thrip life stages
  • AzaMax, an OMRI certified systemic miticide, entering the plants cells where pests with sucking mouthparts feed (primarily mites). This disrupts the reproductive system of the pest that eats the plant and lays eggs in its tissues.
  • For beneficial insects, releases run on a 3-4 week cadence throughout the season, timed to pest pressure rather than a fixed calendar

I don't mulch my squash to minimize squash vine borers, there is nowhere to hide. The goal is threshold-based management: intervene when counts are trending up, not on autopilot.

Soil Health

Everything starts underground. Each spring I incorporate 4-5 cubic yards of fresh compost, about 200 pounds of alfalfa pellets, bone meal into dahlia beds, amending throughout the season as needed. I avoid synthetic fertilizers in favor of slow-release organics and compost teas during the growing season. My dahlias get Alaska Fish Emulsion 1x monthly until they begin to bloom, and once they're green lush they're able to support bloom production so I stop feeding them.

When a plant shows signs of viral infection such as mosaic patterns, stunted growth, color breaking on blooms, it gets removed immediately and it goes into the trash can, not the compost. I treat viral pressure seriously because it spreads through tubers and tools.

In the community garden, I rotate non-dahlia crops through beds between seasons to prevent nutrient depletion and reduce soilborne disease pressure.

Dahlia Culture Practices
  • Pinching: All plants are pinched after the 3rd or 4th node pair to encourage branching. This produces more stems and better vase-life flowers versus letting a single leader run.
  • Staking:Most of the dahlias require heavy staking and I rely on them to support each other.
  • Disbudding: I disbud to one terminal bud per stem.
  • Cutting: I cut very deeply to the bud union on every harvest to keep the plant productive. It can hurt to cut so deeply, but you get more flowers in the long run!
  • Water: Deep, infrequent watering through drip irrigation. Dahlias hate sitting in wet soil but also hate drying out completely during bud formation.
Data-Driven Tracking

As a UC Master Gardener, my approach is rooted in observation and evidence. When something goes wrong, I treat it as data rather than a loss. Every variety in the collection is tracked, status, location, estimated height, bloom form, and field notes, so I can make informed decisions about what to grow again, what to trial at larger scale, and what to retire.

That's what this entire website is: a living, season-by-season record. The collection grid below is pulled live from my growing spreadsheet, so what you see is current as of this season.

Dahlias are a hobby for me. Every single floral arrangement crafted from my cut garden goes directly toward making a difference. I sell seasonal handcrafted bouquets locally, and 100% of the profits are donated monthly to the senior fund of local Golden Retriever Rescue organizations to help fund medical care, supplies, and housing pipelines for senior dogs in need.

Many people ask me if I sell or trade dahlias. Since this is a hobby, I don't sell dahlia tubers or cuttings throughout the year, however, at the end of the season, I dig & divide my tubers for a one time fall tuber sale of things I won't grow anymore or have excess of. Profits of this tuber sale also go to charity. Look out for that tuber sale each November (zone 9B).
